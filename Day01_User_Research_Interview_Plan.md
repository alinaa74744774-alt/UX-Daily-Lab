# [Mini Project] College Textbook Exchange - Interview Plan

> **Research type:** Exploratory, semi-structured user interviews  
> **Planned duration:** 15–20 minutes per participant  
> **Scope:** Current college textbook buying, selling, exchange, and disposal behaviors  
> **Status:** Interview plan only. No interviews have been conducted and no participant data is included.

## Research Goal

Understand how university students currently acquire and pass on used textbooks, including the steps they take, the information they rely on, and the moments that create friction. Use these findings to identify evidence-based opportunities for a campus textbook exchange mini program without assuming which feature students need.

## Target Persona (Fictional)

**“Lin,” a fictional fourth-year undergraduate preparing to move out of campus housing**

- **Context:** Has accumulated textbooks from several semesters and is deciding what to keep, sell, give away, or recycle.
- **Relevant behaviors to explore:** How Lin decides a book is reusable, where Lin looks for buyers or books, how Lin sets expectations about condition and price, and how Lin arranges handoff.
- **Research note:** Lin is a discussion prompt, not a real participant or a claim about all fourth-year students. Recruit students with recent experience buying, selling, exchanging, or trying to pass on a used textbook; do not require them to match this persona exactly.

## Interview Guide

### Opening and consent

“Thanks for speaking with me. I’m learning about how students handle used textbooks. I’m interested in what you actually did, including anything that was inconvenient or unexpected. There are no right or wrong answers. You can skip any question or stop at any time. I’ll take notes without recording identifying details. Is there anything you’d like to ask before we begin?”

Ask about one recent or memorable textbook transaction, attempt, or decision. Do not request names, student IDs, contact details, exact addresses, or other sensitive personal information.

### Question 1 — Explore the current process and behaviors

**Question:** “Please walk me through the most recent time you bought, sold, exchanged, or tried to pass on a used textbook. What happened from the moment you decided what to do until the process was finished?”

**Probe:** “What did you do next at each step, and what information or help did you look for along the way?”

**Listen for:** Trigger, sequence of actions, channels used, decision criteria, other people involved, and workarounds. Do not suggest a particular marketplace or feature.

**Vocabulary in context:** *User research* — the interview gathers evidence about real behaviors before the team decides what to build.

### Question 2 — Explore friction and pain points

**Question:** “Think of a moment in that experience that took more effort than you expected. What was happening, and how did you respond?”

**Probe:** “Can you describe a specific example of what made that moment difficult and what happened afterward?”

**Listen for:** The participant’s own description of a **pain point**, its context, frequency if they volunteer it, its effect, and the workaround used. Do not assume that a difficulty occurred; if the participant reports none, ask what made the process straightforward.

### Question 3 — Explore the mental model and expectations

**Question:** “When you imagine arranging a used textbook exchange through a campus service, what steps would you expect to happen from finding a book or buyer through completing the handoff?”

**Probe:** “At each step, what would you expect the student and the service to do, and what information would help you decide what to do next?”

**Listen for:** The participant’s **mental model** of the process, expectations about book condition and availability, trust and payment assumptions, and what a successful handoff means to them. Treat expectations as hypotheses to investigate, not requirements already validated.

### Neutral follow-up prompts

Use only when clarification would help; allow the participant to answer in their own words.

- “Could you tell me a little more about that?”
- “What happened just before that?”
- “What did you do next?”
- “How did you decide?”
- “What, if anything, would you change about that experience?”

Avoid praise, correction, or feature suggestions that could steer the response. Ask about a concrete past example before exploring hypothetical preferences.

## English Vocab in Action

| Term | Meaning | Application in this plan |
|---|---|---|
| **User Research** | The systematic study of target users to inform product decisions. | “This user research starts with students’ actual textbook exchange behaviors before defining product features.” |
| **Pain Point** | A specific difficulty or frustration in an experience. | “Question 2 asks the participant to describe a specific pain point and its effect without presuming one exists.” |
| **Mental Model** | A person’s beliefs and expectations about how a system or process works. | “Question 3 explores the participant’s mental model of the steps, responsibilities, and information needed for a campus exchange.” |
| **Semi-structured Interview** | An interview using planned open questions while allowing relevant follow-up questions. | “This semi-structured interview uses three core questions and neutral probes to explore unexpected details.” |
| **Qualitative Data** | Non-numerical evidence such as descriptions, quotes, and observed behaviors that helps explain why people act as they do. | “Interview notes are qualitative data; they can suggest patterns but do not establish how common a behavior is among all students.” |

**Vocabulary review:** User Research · Pain Point · Mental Model · Semi-structured Interview · Qualitative Data. Check spelling and explain each term in your own words before using the guide.

## Note-taking and synthesis

For each interview, separate what the participant said from the researcher’s interpretation.

| Topic | Notes |
|---|---|
| Situation and goal | What the participant was trying to do |
| Actions and sequence | What they actually did, in order |
| Evidence and decision criteria | Information they used and why |
| Friction or workaround | The participant’s words, context, and effect |
| Expectations | What they expected to happen at each step |
| Follow-up question | What remains unclear; avoid filling gaps with assumptions |

After interviews, group recurring observations as **themes to investigate**, preserving variation and counterexamples. Do not treat one participant’s comment as proof of a universal need or as a validated product requirement.
